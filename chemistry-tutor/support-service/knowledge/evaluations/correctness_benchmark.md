## 2. [EN] Explain Rutherford's gold foil experiment and what it showed about atomic structure.

**Expected unit:** 1

**Grounded:** True

**Answer:**

Rutherford’s gold‑foil experiment was carried out in 1908‑09 by Ernest Rutherford together with his assistants Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden. A very thin sheet of gold was used as a target and a beam of α‑particles (positively‑charged helium nuclei) from a radioactive source was directed at the foil. The scattered α‑particles were detected on a circular fluorescent screen surrounding the foil.

The observations were:

* **Most α‑particles passed straight through the foil** – they were either completely undeflected or only slightly deflected.
* **A few α‑particles were scattered at large angles** – they changed direction noticeably after passing through the foil.
* **Very few α‑particles bounced back** – they were reflected almost directly toward the source.

To explain these results Rutherford proposed a new atomic model. Because the majority of α‑particles went through with little or no deflection, the atom must be **mostly empty space**. The occasional large‑angle scattering, and especially the rare back‑scattering, could only occur if the α‑particle encountered a **very small, dense region of positive charge** that exerted a strong repulsive force. Rutherford therefore concluded that:

1. **The positive charge of an atom is concentrated in a tiny central core called the nucleus.**  
2. **The nucleus is dense and contains most of the atom’s mass.**  
3. **Electrons occupy the space surrounding the nucleus, leaving the atom largely empty.**

Thus the experiment demonstrated that atoms are not solid indivisible particles (as earlier “plum‑pudding” models suggested) but have a compact, positively‑charged nucleus surrounded by electrons in mostly empty space. This insight formed the basis of the Rutherford‑Bohr (or simply Rutherford) model of the atom.

## 7. [EN] What is dipole moment?

**Answer:**

Dipole moment (µ) is a **vector quantity** that describes the separation of electrical charge within a bond or a molecule.  
It is calculated as  

\[
\mu = \delta \times r
\]

where  

* **δ** = charge of the pole (in coulombs, C)  
* **r** = bond length (in metres, m)

Thus the unit of dipole moment is **C m**. In chemistry it is usually expressed in **Debye (D)**, where  

\[
1\ \text{D} = 3.34 \times 10^{-30}\ \text{C m}
\]

The dipole moment indicates both the magnitude and the direction of the charge separation in a molecule.

## 8. [EN] Explain the difference between ionic bonds and metallic bonds.

**Expected unit:** 2

**Grounded:** True

**Answer:**

**Ionic bonds**

* Formation – An ionic bond is produced when an atom **transfers** one or more valence electrons to another atom. The donor becomes a **cation** and the acceptor becomes an **anion**. The bond is the **electrostatic attraction** between these oppositely‑charged ions.  
* Structure – The ions arrange themselves in a regular three‑dimensional **ionic lattice**.  
* Properties – Because the ions are fixed in place, an ionic solid **does not conduct electricity in the solid state**; it can conduct only when the lattice is melted (or dissolved) where the ions become mobile. Ionic compounds generally have **high melting points** compared with small covalent molecules. (see passage [1]).

**Metallic bonds**

* Formation – In a metallic bond the metal atoms **lose** their valence electrons, which become a **massive delocalised electron cloud** (the “sea of electrons”) surrounding a lattice of **positively charged metal ions**. The attraction between this electron cloud and the cations holds the metal together. (see passage [1]).  
* Factors affecting bond strength –  
  1. **Number of electrons donated** by each atom. For example, Na donates one electron, Mg donates two; more donated electrons give a **stronger metallic bond** (passage [1]).  
  2. **Ionic radius** of the metal cations – larger cation radii lower the electron‑cloud density and give **weaker metallic bonds** (passage [2]).  
  3. **Ionic nature** – the extent to which valence electrons are released; higher ionisation energy reduces the probability of electron release, especially for transition metals (passage [2]).
* Properties – Metals are **excellent conductors of electricity and heat** in both solid and liquid states because the delocalised electrons can move freely. Their melting points vary widely (e.g. Hg = –39 °C, W = 3410 °C) (passage [1]).

## 10. [EN] Explain the difference between empirical formula and molecular formula.

**Expected unit:** 3

**Grounded:** True

**Answer:**

**Empirical formula**

*Definition (from the syllabus material)* – The empirical formula is “the simplest type of formula derived from the composition of the atomic masses of the elements in a compound” and shows the **relative number of atoms of each element** in the substance.  
*Example* – For hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) the empirical formula is **HO**; for ethane (C₂H₆) it is **CH₃**; for benzene (C₆H₆) and ethyne (C₂H₂) it is **CH**.  

Thus the empirical formula tells you the smallest whole‑number ratio of the elements present, but it does **not** necessarily give the actual number of atoms in a single molecule.

---

**Molecular formula**

*Definition (from the syllabus material)* – The molecular formula is “the formula that shows the **actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule** of a compound.”  
*Relation to empirical formula* – The molecular formula is obtained by multiplying the subscripts in the empirical formula by an integer (the factor obtained by dividing the molar mass of the compound by the empirical‑formula mass).  

*Example* – In the worked example for a compound with %Cl = 71.65 %, %C = 24.27 %, %H = 4.07 % and molar mass 98 g mol⁻¹, the empirical formula was found to be **CH₂Cl** (empirical‑formula mass = 49 g mol⁻¹). Because the molar mass (98 g mol⁻¹) ÷ empirical‑formula mass (49 g mol⁻¹) = 2, the molecular formula is **(CH₂Cl)₂** → **C₂H₄Cl₂**.

---

### Key differences

| Aspect | Empirical formula | Molecular formula |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Shows | Smallest whole‑number ratio of atoms | Actual number of atoms in one molecule |
| Obtained from | Percentage composition → moles → ratio → smallest integers | Multiply empirical subscripts by the integer **n** = (molar mass) / (empirical‑formula mass) |
| May be the same as the molecular formula when the integer **n** = 1 | Yes, if molar mass equals empirical‑formula mass | No, if the compound contains more than one empirical unit per molecule |
| Example | **CH₂Cl** (empirical) | **C₂H₄Cl₂** (molecular) |

In summary, the empirical formula gives the simplest ratio of elements, while the molecular formula gives the true composition of a single molecule, obtained by scaling the empirical formula according to the compound’s molar mass.

## 12. [EN] Explain oxidation numbers with an example.

**Expected unit:** 3

**Grounded:** True

**Answer:**

**Oxidation numbers – what they are and how to find them**

An oxidation number is a bookkeeping value that tells us how many electrons an atom has **lost** (positive oxidation number) or **gained** (negative oxidation number) when we imagine that every bond in a molecule is completely ionic.  
It is the charge that the atom would have **if all the shared electrons were assigned to the more electronegative atom** (or split equally when the atoms have the same electronegativity).

Two basic rules are used when assigning oxidation numbers [1]:

1. **Sum rule for a neutral compound** – the sum of the oxidation numbers of all the atoms in a compound equals **zero**.  
2. **Sum rule for an ion** – the sum of the oxidation numbers of all the atoms in an ion equals the **overall charge of the ion**. [4]

Other useful facts from the syllabus:

| Species | Oxidation number |
|---------|------------------|
| Elements in their elemental state (e.g., Na(s), N₂(g)) | 0 |
| Mono‑atomic ions | equal to the ionic charge (e.g., Na⁺ = +1, O²⁻ = ‑2) |
| Fluorine (in any compound) | ‑1 |
| Hydrogen | +1 (except in metal hydrides where it is ‑1) |
| Oxygen | ‑2 (except in peroxides, superoxides, OF₂ etc.) [1] |

---

### Example: Determining the oxidation number of phosphorus in phosphoric acid, **H₃PO₄**

1. **Write the formula and note the overall charge** – H₃PO₄ is a neutral molecule, so the sum of all oxidation numbers must be **0**. [4]

2. **Assign known oxidation numbers**  
   * Hydrogen (H) is always +1 in acids → each H = +1.  
   * Oxygen (O) is normally ‑2 → each O = ‑2.  

3. **Set up the algebraic sum**  

\[
3(\text{oxidation number of H}) + (\text{oxidation number of P}) + 4(\text{oxidation number of O}) = 0
\]

\[
3(+1) + (\text{oxidation number of P}) + 4(-2) = 0
\]

4. **Solve for the unknown (P)**  

\[
3 + (\text{oxidation number of P}) - 8 = 0 \\
(\text{oxidation number of P}) = +5
\]

Hence, the oxidation number of the central phosphorus atom in **H₃PO₄** is **+5**. [4]

---

### What the oxidation number tells us

* The **increase** in oxidation number indicates loss of electrons (oxidation).  
* The **decrease** indicates gain of electrons (reduction).  

In the phosphoric‑acid example, phosphorus has a relatively high positive oxidation number (+5), showing that, compared with elemental phosphorus (oxidation number 0), phosphorus has **lost five electrons** in forming the acid. [1]

This systematic method lets us track electron transfer in redox reactions, balance equations, and identify oxidising and reducing agents. [3]

## 15. [EN] What are coordination compounds of transition metals?

**Expected unit:** 6

**Grounded:** True

**Answer:**

**Coordination compounds of transition‑metal ions are compounds that contain complex ions.**  

A complex ion is built around a **central metal ion** (the transition‑metal cation) to which **two or more surrounding ligands** are attached.  
The ligands are Lewis bases that **donate electron pairs** to the metal ion (a Lewis acid) and form **dative (coordinate) bonds** with it.  

Thus a coordination compound consists of:

* the **complex ion** – e.g. \([Ni(NH_{3})_{6}]^{2+}\) – where the nickel ion is surrounded by six ammonia ligands, and  
* any **counter‑ion(s)** that balance the charge – e.g. the two chloride ions in \([Ni(NH_{3})_{6}]Cl_{2}\).

In summary, coordination compounds of transition metals are those in which a transition‑metal ion forms a complex ion by accepting electron‑pair donors (ligands) around it, the whole assembly being neutralised by appropriate counter‑ions.
